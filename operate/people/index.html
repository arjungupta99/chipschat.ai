People

A workspace is a server. Whoever ran it owns it. Everyone else joins by an invite, gets a role, and is added to spaces the way you would add someone to a group chat — by whoever is already in them. No accounts, no passwords, no email round trip.

Invite somebody

Section titled “Invite somebody”

Only an owner invites. From the You tab, on a computer or the phone, an owner makes a link under the workspace’s own heading: a role attached, with what that role may do under it, once or by several, and good for 30 minutes. On the phone Invite someone sends it out through the share sheet, or shows it as a QR code for somebody beside you to scan with Chips; on a computer, Copy. Send it however you like.

Whoever opens it sees which workspace it is for and as what, types their name, and is in: the browser that opened the link makes a key it keeps to itself, and the invite is what tells the server whose key it is — the same thing a pairing code does for a second device of your own. A single-use link is spent by the first person it lets in; an open one works until it expires or you withdraw it, and the list in You says how many times it has been used.

With a relay, the link opens at the relay’s page, and it asks before it lets anyone in (ADR 0232): the person sees “Waiting for Arjun” and six digits, and your phone, browser or Mac asks “Priya is asking to join Japan trip” with six digits of its own. Ask what they see, and Allow only if the two match. The same goes for a pairing code your device makes for another of your devices. Nothing of the workspace is theirs until you allow it, and a request waits ten minutes.

The link points where the person you send it to can open it: the server’s public address when it has one (CHIPS_PUBLIC_URL), and never 127.0.0.1 when the server is reachable from other machines — a link made on the server’s own machine, or in Chips for Mac, opens elsewhere too.

Joining with the app

Section titled “Joining with the app”

Somebody who has Chips joins in the app rather than a browser (ADR 0164). The link carries the relay’s address and the server’s key beside the token, as a pairing QR does, so the app reaches the server on its network, or through the relay from anywhere else.

  • On a phone, the link goes in by Paste on the first screen, by the camera, or by Open in Chips on the join page Safari shows. The card is the browser’s: who sent it, the workspace, the role, a name, Join.
  • On a Mac, paste it on the Pair screen, or use Open in Chips in the browser.
  • A browser or a Mac already in Chips asks: “You’re already in Demo” if it is one of its workspaces (the link stays unused), or “Join Studio too?”, which joins alongside under the name you go by and switches to it.
  • A phone already in a workspace asks too: “Join Studio too?” joins alongside the one it holds, and the workspace’s name at the top of Spaces switches between them (ADR 0197). Its Join another workspace, under that name, takes a link pasted or a code scanned.

Safari’s page loads only where the server can be reached, which is the tailnet until the relay carries browsers. Off it, paste the link into the app.

Into a space

Section titled “Into a space”

A link can bring somebody straight into a space (ADR 0168). In the space, open its sheet, then Add: Invite someone new, under People, is an owner’s alone. Its form is You’s, but the link makes a Guest of that space (the first choice) or a Member who starts in it, and says what each means there.

  • Whoever opens it reads “Arjun invited you to Bakery in Demo as a guest”, joins, and lands in Bakery. The space says “Priya joined by your link” to you, “by Arjun’s link” to everybody else, and “You joined by Arjun’s link” to her.
  • Somebody already in Demo who opens it is asked, “Join Bakery?”, and joining puts them in the space as you would. The link stays unused, for whoever it was made for. Somebody in the space already is told so and it opens; a guest who is in another space is told a guest is in one.
  • The link goes with the space. Archive or delete the space, or leave it yourself, and the link stops working: the next time anybody uses it, or you look at the list, it is withdrawn. A space locked against changes still takes people in.
  • The open links in You name the space each brings people into.

An admin changes roles and removes people, but invites nobody. The link is shown once: Chips keeps only a digest of the token in it, so a link nobody wrote down cannot be recovered, only remade. The open links are listed under it, each with how often it has been used and until when; Withdraw (on the phone, slide the row left) ends one.

Your name

Section titled “Your name”

You change the name you go by in You, on the desktop or the phone: on the desktop the name at the top is the field — Enter saves it, Escape puts it back — and on the phone a tap on your name opens it in a sheet. It is yours alone to change. It can be up to 120 characters and must be nobody else’s here, since an @ is a name (ADR 0082). Everything you said reads under the new name, and every agent hears it: the socket sends person.renamed.

Roles

Section titled “Roles”

Owner, admin, member, guest — per workspace, as Participant lays them out. An owner or admin changes a role from People in You (on the phone, a person’s page): it asks first, saying what the new role may do. An admin cannot touch an owner’s or make one, and the last owner stays an owner until there is another. Every joining and every change is in the audit: who let whom in, as what, and who changed it.

A guest is in one space at most. The first space they are added to is theirs; a second is refused, and somebody already in several spaces is not made a guest until they are out of all but one. A guest answers an approval only when it names them by id — never as “anyone in the space”. That is the shape for a vendor, or a family member who is there to talk.

In and out of a space

Section titled “In and out of a space”

Joining puts nobody in a space. Somebody in a space adds a person from its sheet (the space’s name, then Add, then their row under People) or with /add @name; a guest is offered only until they are in a space. The person’s app opens the space at once, from its beginning, and the space says who added whom. A member makes nobody else a space’s owner: that takes the space’s owner, or an owner or admin of the workspace.

The space’s owner, or an owner or admin of the workspace who is in the space, takes somebody out: Remove on their row (on the phone, swipe it), which asks first, or /remove @name. They are told where the space was, by whom, until they press OK, and the space says so. Anybody leaves a space with Leave on its sheet, which asks first, or /leave; somebody alone in a space archives or deletes it instead. A guest is offered no Add. The sheet names the workspace’s owners and admins as such. Added back, a person finds the space whole, with what was said while they were out.

Passkeys

Section titled “Passkeys”

A device’s key never leaves the device, which is what makes it safe and what makes it useless on a new phone. A passkey is the way back: made from a device that is already paired — You → Passkeys → Add a passkey, in a browser, where one can be made — and kept by the platform, synced or not, as its own rules say. On a new device, Sign in with a passkey on the pairing screen pairs that device for you, with no code from anywhere.

A passkey is yours alone: nobody else in the workspace, owner included, can see it or remove it. Removing somebody from the workspace withdraws theirs with everything else (below).

Passkeys need a secure address with a name: the server’s own machine as localhost — not 127.0.0.1, which a browser refuses as a passkey’s home — or an https:// address. You offers Passkeys only where one can be made, so it is not there in the desktop app or at an IP address. A Mac mini reached over plain http:// on a LAN takes passkeys on the mini itself and not from the laptop across the room, until it has a TLS address, which Tailscale Serve gives it in one command; the pairing code covers everything else. The origins a passkey may be used from are the server’s public address, loopback, and whatever CHIPS_PASSKEY_ORIGINS names — the desktop app’s, say.

Chips verifies a passkey itself, with the platform’s own cryptography and no dependency: the challenge, the origin, the relying-party id, that the authenticator checked for a person, the signature, and a counter that must move forward. The make of authenticator — attestation — is not checked: a home server has no list to check it against.

Harnesses have owners

Section titled “Harnesses have owners”

Every harness belongs to a named person: the one its approvals default to, whose tokens they are. Whoever added it owns it. An owner hands it on from the harness’s card — to any person in the workspace who is not a guest — and so can a workspace owner or admin, which is how a harness outlives the person who set it up. The handover is in the audit as harness.transferred, and from that moment the new owner’s decisions are the ones policy asks for and the old owner’s tokens screen is closed to them, unless they run the workspace.

The same people may remove a harness from the workspace: Remove harness… on its card or its profile on the phone, or chips remove. Its tokens are withdrawn and its sockets close; it leaves Harnesses, the + and every space’s Add, and can be given no token, space or new space again. What it said stays in its spaces, under its name, and a space that was its home becomes one you can archive or delete. The audit has it as harness.removed. There is no undoing it: to use it again, connect it as a new harness.

A guest of the workspace neither adds a harness nor owns one.

Devices

Section titled “Devices”

Every browser, laptop, phone and CLI a person has paired holds a key of its own, and You lists them: this one first, then by when each was last seen, and those not seen for a month folded under one row with Sign out all. Sign out one you lost — on the phone, push its row left — and it is refused from its next request and its sockets close now. Sign out of this computer (or of this browser, or the phone’s Sign out) withdraws the one you are on and forgets its key; the next visit pairs again, by a code or a passkey. A new device names itself usefully — “Safari on Mac”, “iPhone 15 Pro” — and the phone can give a computer its code: You → Devices → Pair a computer.

Through the API (DELETE /v1/devices/:id), an owner can withdraw anybody’s device by its id, and an admin anybody’s but an owner’s — the rank that keeps an admin from changing an owner’s role keeps them from locking an owner out. A person’s page under You → People lists their devices and when each was last seen, for an owner or an admin. A device is not a person, though: to show somebody out, remove them (below).

Remove somebody

Section titled “Remove somebody”

An owner or admin removes a person from the People list in You: Remove… on their row, and a dialog that says what happens before anything does, Keep or Remove. An admin cannot remove an owner, and nobody removes themselves here: that is deleting your account (below). On the phone, People under the workspace’s name in You opens everyone, and a person’s page has Remove from the workspace…; Invite someone is beside People, for an owner.

Removing somebody, at once:

  • signs them out on every device, and closes their open tabs and sockets: each one says “You’re no longer in Demo” rather than asking them to pair again;
  • withdraws their passkeys, any pairing code waiting for them, and the invites they made that could still be used, so nothing they hold lets them back in;
  • takes them out of every space, each of which says so: “Priya Nair was removed from the workspace by Arjun”;
  • hands the harnesses they own to you, as a handover would.

What they said stays where it was said, under their name. Their name is free again, and the only way back is a new invite, which makes a new person who starts with nothing. The audit has it as person.removed, beside a membership.removed for each space (ADR 0160).

Delete your account

Section titled “Delete your account”

Anybody can delete their own account in a workspace: on the phone, Delete your account under Sign out at the foot of You; on a computer, the Your account card in You. It says what goes and what stays, and Delete wakes once you type the workspace’s name.

  • Goes: your devices and their sign-ins, and push to them; your face; the invites you made.
  • Stays: what you said, in its spaces, under your name. The harnesses you connected go to an owner, as do the approvals only you could answer.

Each space says “Dana left the workspace”, and the audit has it as person.removed with you as the one who did it. The only way back is a new invite, which makes a new person. Signing out is different: it leaves this device and nothing else, and the workspace keeps you.

The only owner can’t delete their account: the workspace is theirs, on the computer it runs on. To delete it, run chips uninstall --remove-data there. To leave it running without you, make someone else an owner first, and then delete your account as anybody does (ADR 0243).

What is where

Section titled “What is where”
Lives in Because
A person’s role The log: person.joined, role.changed, person.removed Who let whom in, and who showed them out, are decisions, and the audit records decisions
A person’s name The registry, recorded as person.renamed A person is identity; the log says when it changed, and to what
An invite The registry, as a digest A token in an exportable log would be a credential in an exportable log
A passkey The registry, as a public key Likewise
A device The registry, as a public key Likewise
