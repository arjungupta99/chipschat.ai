Chips on your phone

The phone is where supervision actually happens. A decision that takes one thumb and no reading gets made in a queue at a coffee shop; the same decision waiting for a laptop gets made an hour later, or not at all. So the phone app is the whole product, not a viewer: the spaces, the fleet, and the two buttons that matter.

The app is on the App Store for iPhone and iPad. Get Chips on your iPhone is the short way in: installing it, the code chips up shows, and the relay for away from home. This page is everything after that. To build the app yourself from a checkout, on your own phone or a simulator, see Building the phone app.

Pair it

Section titled “Pair it”

On the computer that runs Chips, chips up shows a QR code for the phone, and running it again shows a new one. On a computer already paired, You → Devices → Pair another device… shows one, and chips pair --issue prints one in a terminal on the server’s machine. Point the iPhone’s camera at it, or tap Scan a QR code on the app’s first screen. That is the whole of it, because the QR carries the server’s address and the code, and, when the relay is on, the relay and the key to ask for there.

The QR is a link to chipschat.ai/p with all of that in the part after #, which a browser never sends anywhere. With Chips installed, iOS opens the link in Chips. Without it, the page there says where to get Chips and, once it is installed, hands the code to it with Open in Chips. The page reads nothing from the link and sends nothing.

A browser can’t pair from a phone’s QR: a browser makes its device key only on a secure address, and your server’s address on a home network or a tailnet is not one. On a laptop, open Chips at http://127.0.0.1:4431 on the machine that runs it, or put the server behind HTTPS, which Tailscale Serve does in one command.

The address in that QR comes from the server, not from the browser drawing it. A browser only knows the origin it loaded from, which is 127.0.0.1 on the machine chipsd runs on, or a dev server on another port — and 127.0.0.1 names the one device in the room that is not holding the camera. So the server says where it answers: what CHIPS_PUBLIC_URL was set to, else the address it bound, else the network it is on. Where a machine is on two networks at once, a home Wi-Fi and a tailnet, it picks the home one, because that is where the phone in the room usually is.

That address is the direct way in. Away from that network, the phone uses the relay, and the QR carries one only once the relay is on: Chips’s own after an owner turns on Use the relay, or chips up --reach relay does, and one of your own, set with CHIPS_RELAY_URL, from the start. Then a phone reaches Chips from anywhere, with no VPN on the phone and nothing forwarded on your router. See the relay and away from home.

Tailscale is a direct path beside the relay. chips up --reach tailnet puts the tailnet’s address in the QR, and a phone with the Tailscale app connected reaches it at home and away.

If the QR will not scan, tap Type a pairing code instead, or Have a pairing code? on the first screen, and enter the code and the server’s address by hand: six characters say nothing about where the server is.

A code is single use and expires. If pairing fails, issue a fresh one.

With an invite instead

Section titled “With an invite instead”

Somebody who was sent an invite joins on the phone without a computer (ADR 0164). On the first screen, Have an invite link? pastes a link that was copied, Scan a QR code takes one shown as a QR (an owner’s Invite someone shows the link’s QR beside it), and Open in Chips on the join page in Safari hands it over. Each opens the join card: who sent it, the workspace, the role and what it may do, a name, and Join. When the relay is on, the link carries it and the server’s key, so this works off the server’s network too, and the card says when it went through the relay. Without it, the link opens only where the server’s own address does, and the invite says so. A link that was used, withdrawn or has expired says so, with who to ask.

A phone already in a workspace is asked first, on a sheet over wherever it was. The same workspace says “You’re already in Acme” and leaves the link unused. Another says “Join Studio too?”, and joining puts Studio beside Acme on this phone (ADR 0197): the workspace’s name at the top of Spaces switches between them, and its Join another workspace takes the next link, pasted or scanned.

A link to a space (ADR 0168) names it on the card (“Arjun invited you to Bakery in Demo as a guest”), and joining opens the space. In the workspace already, the sheet is about the space: “Join Bakery?” puts you in it and leaves the link unused, “You’re already in Bakery” opens it, and a guest in another space is told a guest is in one. An owner makes such a link from the space’s sheet: Add → Invite someone new, the last row under People.

chips://join?invite=…&lan=…&relay=…&key=… opens the same screens, which is how to try them on a simulator: xcrun simctl openurl booted "chips://join?invite=…&lan=http%3A%2F%2F127.0.0.1%3A4598".

Away from home

Section titled “Away from home”

Away from home the phone reaches Chips through the relay, which an owner turns on: You, then Relay under the workspace’s name, then Use the relay. Until then, a phone away from home has no way to the server, and the setup card’s Away from home says so with Set up, which opens the same page. A phone already paired learns of the relay from the server, on its next connection or at once while it is connected, so nobody scans again.

A phone holding both addresses tries the network first, on a short timeout, and falls back to the relay on its own. It never asks which network you are on, and there is no setting for it. When it goes through the relay the header says Connected through the relay, so you always know which way you got in.

If the relay answers and your server is not registered with it, the phone says your server is not reachable — it may be asleep, rather than Reconnecting…. That is a different instruction: a dropped network is worth waiting two seconds for; a laptop that shut its lid is worth going home and opening, and the relay cannot help, because there is nothing awake on the far side to reach. See when your machine sleeps.

Getting around

Section titled “Getting around”

Four tabs at the foot of the screen: Spaces, Agents, Search and You, with the + for a new space beside them. The app opens on Spaces, and an ask is answered in its space. The bar is the phone’s own, so it shrinks out of the way as a list goes up and comes back as it comes down, and a press feels like every other tab bar on the phone. A development build has a fourth tab, Runtime, which is a report and not part of the app.

A space, an ask or a harness opens over the whole screen with a bar of its own: the chevron takes you back, and a space’s bar says what the space is called with, underneath, the harness working in it — or how many people are in it, where there are more than the name can hold.

On an iPad, in a window 700 points wide or more, the app stands in columns instead (ADR 0256, ADR 0276): the tab’s list, what a row in it opens, and a third column one level deeper. The four tabs and the gold + are a bar across the foot of the list, level with a space’s message field. Held upright with a third column open, the bar folds to the round of the tab in front; press it to open the four out again.

For the workspace’s owner, Spaces starts with a card while setting up is unfinished (ADR 0170): six steps — Chips running, a computer paired, the first agent’s hello, this phone, notifications, and a way to reach Chips from away — ticked from what is already true, each step left with the thing to do beside it. When the last is ticked it says You’re set up and folds away; the × puts it away on this phone.

Spaces is a list of spaces, each a row with the space’s face, its name, the time it was last live and the last thing said in it. A number on the right of a row is how many lines in it you have not read. A space waiting on a yes says Needs a yes instead of quoting itself.

Search finds a space, an agent or something said. Spaces are found by their names and by who is in them, agents by their names and by their harness’s, both on the phone as you type; messages, voice notes’ words and the steps of runs come from the server once you have typed two letters and paused. What matched is marked in gold. All shows three spaces and three agents, then the messages; the chips under the field show one kind at a time. A space opens, an agent opens its page, and a message opens its space already at that message, however far up, lit for a moment with the words you searched for marked in it. With the server out of reach, spaces and agents are still found, and the messages say why they aren’t. What you searched for is kept on this phone under Recent: tap one to run it again, × to forget it. With nothing typed, the faces of your spaces and your agents (with their pins) drift slowly up behind the recents and the empty tab, each in a softly breathing glow of its own colour, and go when you type; with Reduce Motion on, they stand still. Before you have searched for anything, the tab says Find and a word that rolls over from spaces to agents to anything said, with the whole sentence under it; with Reduce Motion on, it stays on spaces.

Agents is every harness in the workspace, one card each: what it is doing, how far it is trusted, how many spaces it works in, and how much of its context and its budget have gone. A harness says what it is doing only while it is doing something. One that is idle, or that has finished, says nothing at all — the reply in the space is the proof that it finished, and a screen of green Done labels is a fleet that looks busy while doing nothing.

You is a settings list, the way the phone’s own Settings is one: your face, name and role at the top (tap it to change the name you go by), then Appearance, Notifications, the workspace’s own settings if you are an owner or an admin, and About — the server, how much the phone keeps, and which build it is. Devices and Sign out are at the foot, one above the other, and Delete your account under them (People). Every row says what it is set to, and a row with a chevron opens its setting in a sheet.

Under the workspace’s name, an owner finds Relay (away from home), and an owner or an admin finds People: everyone in the workspace, the longest here first, with their role and who let them in. A person’s page shows their role, which changes from a sheet that asks first (Guest is off for somebody in more than one space), the spaces you share with them, and Remove from the workspace…, which asks in the same words as the computer. Invite someone, an owner’s alone, makes a link: as what, and once or by several, for 30 minutes; the link is shown once, with its QR code, Share… and Copy, and the open links are listed under it, each sliding left on Withdraw.

In Devices, this phone comes first and the rest by when each was last seen; those not seen for a month are folded under one row. Push a device left to sign it out: it is refused from its next request. Pair a computer makes a code and a link: Send the link shares it, and opened on the computer it pairs Chips for Mac from any network, through the relay (the /pair page). Typing the code in Chips works too, on the server’s own network. Leave the sheet open and it says when the computer has arrived. Sign out, at the foot, withdraws this phone at the server first, then forgets its key.

In a space

Section titled “In a space”

A space reads from the bottom, like every chat. What you send rises into its place, and what the harness sends does the same; if you have scrolled up to read, a line landing below does not move what you are reading, and the space follows the end only when you are already there. Your own message is the exception: sending one takes you to the foot of the space, where it is landing, from wherever in the history you were.

While the harness works, a bubble from it sits under the newest line: three dots, then what it is thinking and which tool it has in hand, as the harness reports them. It is one line and the same height throughout, so the space does not move while the words in it change. It goes the moment the answer lands — and it does not come at all while a lane’s own row above it is already breathing, which is the same waiting said once. The calls it made are under the answer afterwards, folded, one line saying how many.

The field grows with your words, up to six lines or so, and shrinks back when you send. Send is the blue circle inside the right edge of the field, dim until there is something to send. What you typed and did not send stays with the space while the app runs, so a space left mid-sentence to check something in another is a space come back to with the sentence still there.

The phone keeps its own copy of the workspace’s log, so a space opens at once, offline too, and the copy is the workspace’s: it stays through an unpairing and a pairing again. When the server lists a space the copy never saw opened, the phone reads that space again from its beginning as it connects, so a hole in the copy closes itself; and the phone resumes from a mark it records only behind what it has safely written, so a copy from before the mark was kept is read again from the start once, and a write that fails is written again rather than skipped past.

Reading a space

Section titled “Reading a space”

Your messages sit on the right in teal, everyone else’s on the left. Messages one person says close together close up into a turn, with the time inside the last of them, and a line saying the day and the hour stands over the first message and after any silence of a quarter of an hour.

A lane a harness routed to one of its Spaces is one row in the space: the two faces, and the Space the work went to named beside them — three dots breathing at its right edge while the work is going, Failed if it ended badly, and nothing once it is done. Files the work came back with are under the row: a lane asked for photographs answers with the photographs, in the space you asked in, and not only in the space it did the work in. Tap the row to go to the other end of the lane; press and hold it for what was asked, its bounds, what it cost and what came back. A lane with no other end — a Space handing work to a part of itself, Chess Game → Engineer, in the one space — opens those details on the tap, since there is nowhere to cross to. In the specialist’s space the same lane is the faces alone, over your own words and on their side: the space is already named after the last face and the words under it are already the first one’s, so the names — and the via that used to sit under the bubble — are left to a screen reader there.

What the space is waiting on is a card at its foot, under the newest line: an approval with Approve and Deny, and a question with its form on the card itself — the options, a field for your own words where the harness takes them, and Send the answer. A question is answered there, under the message it asks about, and not on a screen of its own. A harness may ask and end its turn at once, as Jacobian does; the answer still reaches it. When the server turns an answer or a decision away, the card says why in red — Not sent: that question was already answered by Dana — rather than looking as if nothing happened.

Press and hold any message and it lifts out of the space: Copy, Select, Share (or Edit, on your own) and Pin above it; below it, Not right? on an agent’s words, which asks for a reason or a few words and sends them to the agent to look again, the deletions, and, on the message that closed a run, What the run did: the steps, how long they took, what the run cost, and the model and tokens when the harness said them. The receipt is never drawn in the space itself.

The app follows the text size set in Settings → Display & Brightness → Text Size, and the accessibility sizes above it. A message stops growing at half again its size, so that the time under it and the name over it stay on their own lines; everything outside a bubble follows the setting the whole way. For more than that, iOS’s own Zoom magnifies the screen.

Sending a photo, a video or a file

Section titled “Sending a photo, a video or a file”

The + beside the composer opens a sheet: Photo or video from your library, Take a photo or video, or A file, which is the Files app. What you pick appears as a chip above the field, uploading as it arrives — the message goes the moment you send it, because the bytes already have — and the × takes one out. Where a photo or a video was taken is stripped on the server, as it is for the desktop; the desktop’s Keep location box is not on the phone.

Tap a photo’s chip to look at it before it goes. It grows out of the chip onto the black stage, where it pinches, zooms and drags as a sent photo does; the bottom says its name, its size and Not sent yet, and Send stands exactly where it stands in the composer, dimmed while the photo is still uploading. Send from there and the message goes; Close, a drag down or the back gesture put the photo back in its chip, still waiting.

Voice note is the sheet’s fourth row. Tap the microphone to record, and tap again to stop and add it; a note stops by itself at five minutes. Its chip says uploading and transcribing… while the server writes down what was said, and the message carries the sound and the words, so a harness hears both. The space shows a voice note as a round Play and the words under it, and search finds it by them. The row needs a build of the app made since voice notes went in; an older one shows the words and opens the sound through the share sheet.

A message’s photos, and the videos the server measured, arrive as one album under the bubble, in the order they were sent. A photo alone keeps its own shape and stops at 200 points tall, so a portrait receipt does not take the screen; two sit side by side; three are one tall beside two; four or more are two by two, with +2 on the fourth for the ones past it. A video is a tile of the album like any photo: its poster, a play mark and how long it runs, and a tap opens the viewer on it, where it plays. One the server could not measure is a row under the album whose Play does the same. An HTML file — a harness’s mock, a report — opens as a page over the space, with Done and the file’s name at the top and Share beside them. The page runs its own scripts, so a mock’s switches work, and it can reach nothing of the app’s: no files, no key, no storage that outlives it. A link you tap in it opens in Safari, or Mail for an email address; a page that tries to leave on its own, send a form, or open a chips:// link stays where it is. A page over 5 MB goes to the share sheet instead, and so does every HTML file on a build of the app made before pages went in. Anything else is a row that opens on a tap into the share sheet — Quick Look, Save to Files, and every app that takes the file. A file is fetched through the phone’s own signature and kept once, so a second look costs no request.

What a tap on a photo or a video does

Section titled “What a tap on a photo or a video does”

The photo grows out of its tile to fill the screen, over a black stage, and the bars come in a moment after: Close at the top left, where it stands in the deck (3 of 8), Share and ⋯ at the top right, and who sent it, when, its name, its size and its pixels along the bottom.

  • The deck is the photos and videos of the twenty messages around the one you tapped, in the order they were said: the space’s last twenty if you tapped near the foot, and ten either side if you tapped further up. An album’s photos are all in it, even the ones past the four the grid shows. Voice notes and files are not; they stay rows in the space.
  • Swipe left or right for the next or the previous. The ends hold, and nothing wraps.
  • Pinch to zoom, up to three times or to the photo’s own pixels, whichever is more, and drag to move around while zoomed. Double-tap zooms to two and a half times where you tapped, and double-tap again fits it back. A pinch let go at under three quarters of the size closes it.
  • Drag down (or up), and the space shows through behind as the photo shrinks. Past a line you feel as a click, let go and it flies back into its tile; let go before the line and it comes back. Close, and the back gesture or Android’s back button, do the same.
  • Tap once to hide the bars, and again to bring them back.
  • Press and hold, or ⋯, for the menu: Share, Save into Photos (or the gallery on Android), Copy the picture, Go to message, which closes the viewer onto the message and flashes it, Details, and Open original. The viewer shows a copy made for the screen, and Open original swaps in the file as it was sent; it is not offered while the file itself is what is showing: a GIF, a photo the server could not read, or anything from a server from before 15 September 2026, which makes no such copy.
  • A photo that would not come keeps its thumbnail and says why, with Try again.

A video in the viewer is an item like any other: you swipe to it and away, and drag it down to close. It is not zoomed.

  • It plays when you land on it, from the start, and pauses when you swipe away. Swipe back and it goes on from where it was.
  • On your own network or tailnet it streams: it starts in about a second, and you can move through it at once, while the rest comes as it plays. A spinner shows while it starts, or while it catches up after you move. Nothing is kept on the phone.
  • Through the relay it downloads first: its poster shows a ring with how much has come, and the bars say how many megabytes of how many. The whole copy comes to the phone before it plays, and is kept, so a second look starts at once. So does a server from before 15 September 2026, wherever you are.
  • The bars carry its controls: play and pause, a line you drag along to move through it, the time, mute, and Picture in Picture. They go by themselves three seconds into playing. Tap while they are hidden, or while it is paused, to play or pause it and bring them back; tap while it plays with them showing to hide them.
  • Dragging it down pauses it; if it comes back, it plays on.
  • System player, in the menu, hands it to the phone’s own full-screen player, where AirPlay and captions are. On Android that is the player’s own controls, with speed, and captions when the video has them.
  • The server makes a copy that plays everywhere a little after a video arrives. Until it exists the original plays, and the bars say the copy that plays everywhere is being made. A server from before 14 September 2026 does not say, and the bars say nothing.
  • An original this phone cannot play, such as an iPhone’s HDR video on an Android phone, keeps its poster until the copy is made. It says This phone cannot play the original and the copy that plays everywhere is being made, with Try again, which plays the copy once it exists.
  • On Android, Picture in Picture pins the video alone, in a window shaped like it, and the status bar hides with the bars.

On an iPad or an Android tablet

Section titled “On an iPad or an Android tablet”

The viewer is the same, sized to the window. Where the window is wide — at least 768 points across and 600 down, which is an iPad or an Android tablet held either way, or a foldable opened — it covers the whole window, the list and the space beside it both, and adds two things:

  • A filmstrip under the caption: a thumbnail of everything in the deck, the one on the stage ringed in gold and kept in the middle as you go. Tap one to go straight there. A video’s has a play mark. It goes with the bars, so a zoomed photo or a playing video has the stage to itself.
  • Arrows at the left and right, for the one before and the one after, with none at an end. They rest a little dim and come up under a mouse or a trackpad’s pointer. A swipe still works.

Turn the tablet and the picture fits the new shape. A zoomed photo keeps its zoom and keeps what was in the middle of the screen in the middle, so the line of a receipt you were reading is still the one in front of you.

A narrow window is the phone’s viewer, with no strip and no arrows: an iPad app in a split or Slide Over, a small Stage Manager window, or a tablet app shrunk to a third. Making the window narrow while the viewer is up closes the space and the viewer with it, and puts you back on the list.

With Reduce Motion on, the photo fades in and out where it stands instead of flying. With VoiceOver, the photo is one element named for it — Photo 3 of 8, site-02.jpg, from Dana, today at 11:02 — and swiping up or down moves through the deck. Save asks once for permission to add to your library, and never to read it.

How big a file may be is the workspace’s to say: an owner or an admin sets the caps on photos, videos and files in You → the workspace’s name → Attachment limits, in megabytes, under the server’s own limit. Nobody else sees that group. A file over its cap is refused at the chip, before a byte of it leaves the phone, in the words the server would have used.

Being told

Section titled “Being told”

The phone asks the first time it matters: when the first ask from an agent arrives, a sheet says Hear when an agent needs you? with Turn on notifications, which brings iOS’s own alert, and the sheet’s X for not now. It asks once; after that it is yours to turn on here:

You → Notifications → Notify me on this phone. Turn it on and the phone asks iOS for permission, gets a push token, and tells your server where to reach it. From then on, a decision that needs you lights the lock screen within a few seconds, with Approve and Deny on it. Approve asks for Face ID first. Neither opens the app. On Android, where a notification’s button cannot ask for the unlock, the button reads Approve… and opens the ask once the phone is unlocked, and you approve on the card. Deny still answers from the lock screen.

The rows under the switch are the ones that decide who hears what, each with a line under it saying what it is about. Decisions — an approval, or a question with no default answer, that an agent is stopped on — come right away, or are off. Quiet hours are a window set on the phone’s own time wheel, and whether a decision still gets through. Agents is one choice per harness, covering every agent it runs: Everything (its decisions, and messages that @ you), Decisions (only those), or Nothing; the sheet says under each harness what its choice lets through. They live on the server and are yours alone, so the phone and the desktop app obey the same ones — and you are told once, on one device, not once per screen you own. The phone is pushed decisions, messages that @ you, and what other people write in your spaces, since every space starts on People’s messages; each space’s sheet changes that, and an agent’s reply reaches the phone only from a space set to Every message (Notifications). Finished work and checkpoints are the desktop’s to raise, and it has the settings for them.

Quiet hours are worked out in this phone’s time zone, not the server’s. A phone in Kolkata and a laptop in California belonging to the same person are night and day apart, and the server knows which is which.

What a push carries

Section titled “What a push carries”

Three ids and two fixed sentences: A decision is waiting / Approve or deny from here. It does not carry the action, the preview, the risk classes, the harness’s name or the space’s title. Chips has no Apple or Google push credentials of its own, so a push goes from your server through the Chips push relay, which holds the Chips app’s Expo access token, then through Expo’s service on the way to your phone. What they are handed is a token, those two sentences and an opaque identifier. The words appear only when you open the card, over your phone’s own authenticated connection. ADR 0045 records the reasoning.

The cost is real and worth saying out loud: a Chips notification cannot be triaged from the lock screen the way a mail notification can. You are told that you are needed, not what for.

What push needs

Section titled “What push needs”

A push reaches a lock screen only through Apple, and Apple delivers only to a build of the app that carries the push entitlement. The app from TestFlight does. A build of your own needs a paid Apple team and the app’s APNs key with Expo (push on a build of your own).

Your server needs nothing. The Chips app’s Expo project has enhanced security on, so Expo takes its pushes only with the project’s access token. A server without it sends its pushes through the Chips push relay at relay.chipschat.ai, which has the token and passes them on to Expo (ADR 0246). The relay checks that each is a Chips push, pushes one phone at most thirty times a minute, logs no token or words, and counts how many it sent each day. A server with the token (CHIPS_PUSH_ACCESS_TOKEN) posts to Expo itself. A build of the app under your own Apple team pushes through an Expo project of your own, so the server it pairs with needs that project’s token (push, with a paid team).

A server that can’t push leaves the banner to your computer (ADR 0244). That is one pointed at Expo, with CHIPS_PUSH_URL, without the token, or one whose token Expo refused. The phone says so under the switch, the setup card leaves Notifications undone with your server can’t send them yet, the phone doesn’t ask to turn them on, and chips doctor says it on the server’s machine. What needs you still waits in Chips.

If the switch will not turn on

Section titled “If the switch will not turn on”

It says what is wrong rather than failing quietly:

  • This build has no Expo project id. A build from this checkout has Chips’ own, in app.json; a build under your own Apple team needs one of its own (push, with a paid team).
  • This phone is not allowing notifications. Settings → Notifications → Chips.
  • This build cannot receive push. It was signed by a free team, so it has no push entitlement and iOS will not issue a token. The switch stays off until the build changes: the fix is a paid team.
  • This phone could not get a push token, and why, in iOS’s words or Expo’s — a dropped connection, usually. Try again.
  • The server did not take it, and what it said.
  • Your server can’t send notifications, with the switch on or off: it has no access token for Expo, or Expo refused the one it has (above). The switch still works, and the phone is pushed to the day the server can.
  • Your server’s last notifications didn’t get through: the service that delivers them, Expo or the Chips relay, didn’t answer or didn’t accept them. The next one tries again, and the line goes once one gets through. A decision the phone missed comes up on your computer instead.

What is not there yet

Section titled “What is not there yet”

Android. The app has been walked on an Android emulator, not on a phone, and push on Android waits for a Firebase project for Expo’s service to send through. Building the phone app says how to build it.

Passkeys. Signing in with a passkey is not built on the phone. When it is, it needs the Associated Domains entitlement, which a free team cannot sign either, and a server at an HTTPS address the app can name. Pairing with a code is how you get in on a phone.
