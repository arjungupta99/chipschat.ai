The relay

On the network your server is on, a phone reaches it directly, and nothing here is involved: the QR chips serve prints carries the server’s address on that network, and pairing and everything after it go straight to it. Away from that network nothing works at all — a home router does not accept a connection nobody asked for, and a phone on cellular is behind the carrier’s NAT as well. Neither end can be dialled. So both dial out, to a relay, which holds the two connections together and forwards bytes it cannot read (ADR 0038).

A phone holding both addresses tries the network first, on a short timeout, and falls back to the relay on its own. It never asks which network you are on, and there is no setting for it.

Run your own with the one command below, or use the one Chips hosts at relay.chipschat.ai, which is free and has terms of its own. It serves any server that proves its key.

The Chips relay

Section titled “The Chips relay”

Every server has the Chips relay behind its owner’s switch, off until an owner turns it on (ADR 0245). Turning it on sends the relay your server’s key and address, so it waits for you to choose:

  • In the app: You → Relay on the Mac and the web, You → Workspace → Relay on the phone. The setup card’s Away from home takes you there.
  • From the terminal: chips up --reach relay, or pick Your devices, anywhere, through the Chips relay when chips up asks who can reach Chips. It is the first answer on a machine with no tailnet. Chips stays reachable directly on your Wi-Fi as well, so a phone at home doesn’t go the long way round.

A server you give a relay of your own, with CHIPS_RELAY_URL, has that one behind the switch instead, and on until you turn it off.

Notifications through the Chips relay

Section titled “Notifications through the Chips relay”

The Chips relay also sends notifications to the Chips app for servers that can’t send them themselves (ADR 0246). Expo, which delivers them, takes the app’s notifications only with its project’s access token, and the relay has it. A server without the token posts its notifications to https://relay.chipschat.ai/v1/push, whether or not its relay switch is on, and the relay passes them on to Expo. This opens no way into your server, so there’s no switch for it.

The relay takes only a Chips notification: to an Expo push token, with a Chips notification’s fields, at most 4 KiB. It sends one phone at most thirty a minute, and takes at most three hundred a minute from one address. It logs no token, sentence or id, and counts how many it sent each day. A server with the token, CHIPS_PUSH_ACCESS_TOKEN, posts to Expo itself, and CHIPS_PUSH_URL sends them anywhere else.

The relay’s GET /health says what became of the last notifications it passed on, as pushes: ready, refused when Expo turned down its token, failing when Expo didn’t answer or didn’t take them, or no_access_token when the relay has no push door. It changes only when Expo answers, so a relay nobody has pushed through yet says ready.

Run one

Section titled “Run one”

That is the whole relay: one port, two doors — /v1/serve for a server dialling out, /v1/connect for a phone dialling in — and GET /health beside them. It also serves the page an invite link opens at, /join, since a server with a relay sends its invites as that page’s link: the relay is on the internet where the server may not be, and the invite stays in the link’s fragment, which never reaches the relay. The same goes for /pair, the page a pairing link opens at: Pair a computer on a phone sends one, and so does Copy link under a code in You → Devices. On a computer it shows the code and offers Open in Chips, which pairs Chips for Mac through the relay when the server’s network is out of reach, or Pair in this browser on the server’s network. It says when the code has run out. Beside it is the file that lets the iPhone app open those links itself (/.well-known/apple-app-site-association). Update a relay along with its servers, so the page is there for their links. CHIPS_RELAY_HOST and CHIPS_RELAY_PORT say where it listens (127.0.0.1, 4440); CHIPS_RELAY_MAX_SESSIONS_PER_SERVER how many phones it holds open for one server (32). Put TLS in front of it before the internet does — a Caddy line is enough — so the control frames, which name keys and addresses, cross under wss://. The tunnel inside is ciphertext either way.

A relay on the internet serves any server that proves its key, unless you give it a list. CHIPS_RELAY_ALLOW_KEYS names the servers it serves by their relay keys, comma-separated, and it turns any other away with not_allowed:

A server’s relay key is relay.key in its GET /v1/health once it has CHIPS_RELAY_URL, asked on the server’s own machine (curl -s http://127.0.0.1:4431/v1/health): from anywhere else health names neither the relay nor the key. A server a relay turns away says the same key in its log, with what to do, and keeps dialling, so it is served as soon as the relay has the key.

Run that proxy on the relay’s machine. The relay tells your server each phone’s address, for the limit on wrong answers, and counts by it the phones one address holds open for a server. On a connection from loopback it takes that address from X-Forwarded-For, and only from its last entry, the one the proxy wrote. Entries before it are whatever the phone sent. From a proxy on another machine the header is not read, and every phone counts as that machine.

A relay of your own has no push door unless you give it the Chips app’s Expo access token (CHIPS_PUSH_ACCESS_TOKEN), and only Chips has that. Servers using your relay still send their notifications through the Chips relay’s push door, unless they have the token themselves.

Then tell the server where it is, and restart it:

chips serve prints a relay line, and every pairing code it prints from then on — and every one issued from You → Devices → Pair another device…, or chips pair --issue — carries the relay’s address and the server’s tunnel key in the QR, beside the code. A phone that scans it can reach the server from anywhere. A device paired before the server had a relay learns of it from the server on its next connection, or at once when an owner turns the relay on while it is connected (ADR 0247), and keeps it, so nobody pairs again (ADR 0144). The same goes for a server whose relay moves.

On a box of its own

Section titled “On a box of its own”

The files for a relay on a small Linux VM are in packaging/relay/: a systemd unit that runs chips relay as its own user on loopback, a Caddyfile, an env file for the list, journald kept for seven days, and a cloud-init.yaml that puts them all in place. Its README is the steps, from an empty VM to a relay answering GET /health.

What the relay sees, and what it cannot

Section titled “What the relay sees, and what it cannot”

The relay learns which server spoke to which phone, from which address, and when, because a switchboard must. It logs that only at debug, which is off: at info it says how many servers and sessions it holds, once a minute when that changes, and the key of a server it turned away. On the box in packaging/relay/, the journal is deleted after seven days. At the end of each UTC day it logs how many servers it listed, how many a phone came through to, how many sessions, and the bytes each way, and with CHIPS_RELAY_COUNTS_FILE set it keeps that line: numbers, with no key and no address in them. It reads no event, holds no role and enforces nothing: who may do what is the server’s door, exactly as on the network. It cannot open the bytes it moves.

Those bytes are a tunnel between the phone and the server: a Noise NK handshake against the server’s key from the QR, so the phone knows it is talking to its server and not to whoever the relay put behind that key; then every request and the socket, as messages inside it, signed by the phone’s device key the way every request has been signed since the first release (ADR 0039). The test suite captures every byte the relay forwards and checks that none of the words, paths, codes or headers is in it.

A server proves it holds its key before the relay lists it, so nobody can sit where your server should be. A phone that asks for a key nobody holds is told so and closed — which is what a phone shows when the laptop at home is asleep: the relay cannot conjure a server that is not running.

Limits

Section titled “Limits”

A tunnel is a stranger until a credential lets it in, so the server allows thirty requests without one per tunnel per minute — enough for pairing, an invite or a passkey sign-in, and not enough to guess a code — and the pairing limiter counts by the address the relay saw, an IPv6 one by its /64, as on your own domain. The relay holds at most thirty-two phones per server, and sixteen of them from any one address, counted the same way. A server’s key is in every pairing QR and invite link, and anyone who has it can ask the relay for that server; this keeps one address from taking all thirty-two. Sixteen, not two, because a household away together, or a team in one office, shares an address.

The relay takes no message over 256 KiB (a tunnel message is at most 64 KiB) and holds only so much for an end that is slow to take it. Past 4 MiB waiting for one side, it stops reading the other until the first is down to 1 MiB, as TCP would have slowed the sender with no relay in between; past 16 MiB it ends that session (too_slow), and the phone reconnects. One socket carries all of a server’s phones, so a phone that stops reading slows the others of the same server until it catches up or goes. A relay lists at most eight servers from any one address, counted the same way, and turns a ninth away as busy: a home or an office runs one or two, and a relay open to anyone needs a limit on what one address can take. Homes behind a carrier’s shared address (CGNAT) count as one address, so on such a connection the eight can fill with servers that aren’t yours. A server turned away keeps dialling and is listed when there is room, and a relay of your own has no such neighbours.

NAT traversal — the two ends learning each other’s public addresses and speaking directly, with the relay as the fallback — is deferred behind a number rather than a date: it is revisited when a relay’s egress passes 10 TB in a month or its bill passes $100. Every frame and every pairing payload already carries a version and a candidate-address list, empty for now, so that day is a new field and not a re-pairing of every phone.
