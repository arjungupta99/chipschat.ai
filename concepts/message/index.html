Message

Text is one kind of message. The rest are what make a harness space different from a group chat: they are structured, so they can be tapped on a phone, routed to a teammate, and read back in an audit.

Type Sent by Carries Shows up as
text Anyone Markdown, mentions, reactions, attachments A bubble, with media inline
status Harness Phase, a verb phrase, progress, an estimate A pill under the harness’s name
approval Harness Action, preview, risk class, expiry, who may answer A card with Approve, Edit, Deny
question Harness Prompt, options, whether free text is allowed, a default A card with tappable options
receipt Harness Steps, tool calls, sources, tokens, cost, duration A collapsed line that expands inline
artifact Harness File, document, code, image or table, with a version A rich card that opens in the side panel
task A person, or a lead Goal, constraints, deadline, autonomy level A card that becomes a run, or a lane
handoff Anyone Recipient, context summary, open items A card; the recipient is pinged
checkpoint Harness Milestone reached, what is next A compact card, FYI tier
summary Harness Outcome, what changed, cost, follow-ups A card at the end of a run
system Chips Joins, policy changes, budget alerts, the kill switch A muted centred line

Any of them can carry attachments: photos, video, voice notes, files. Anything you can send a person, you can send a harness — a harness receives them as signed URLs that expire in ten minutes and are bound to its own token. Which Markdown draws, and what each kind of file opens as, is in What Chips draws.

A text says whom it is for. @Ledger in the text is a mention: Chips resolves it against the space’s members as the message arrives and fills in mentions with the participant ids — a value the sender writes is replaced — and every screen draws the same chip. A handle is a name without its spaces, or its first word, and is not case-sensitive; @all names every harness in the room. From the mentions, the sender’s to, a reply and the room, Chips then decides which harnesses the message is for and fills in addressed_to (ADR 0081): those harnesses hear it as message.new and take a turn, and the others hear it as message.overheard. The rules are in Several harnesses and people in one space.

Threads

Section titled “Threads”

On the wire a text can grow a thread: a space of its own with a parent — space.created with kind: "thread", parent_space_id and root_message_id — and no members of its own: whoever is in the room is in its threads, sees them, and leaves them with the room. A message roots one thread; a thread is not opened inside a thread, in a closed space, or from a message taken back. A reply is the room’s news: the room comes forward in the list and its unread count moves (ADR 0035).

Neither client offers to open one, for now (15 September 2026): Reply in a thread, the desktop’s thread panel and the reply count under a line are withdrawn until a thread earns its place in a room read like Messages. The log keeps the threads already opened, search still reads them, and a harness may still open one through the door.

Edit, take back, pin

Section titled “Edit, take back, pin”

A message is changed by events, and the log keeps the words. Its author — a person or a harness — may change them with message.edited: the room shows the latest, marked edited, and the audit says who. Its author, the space’s owner, or a workspace owner or admin may take it back with message.deleted: a tombstone stands where the bubble was, its files, thumbs and pin go with it, and it takes nothing more — no edit, no reaction, no thread. The message.post that carried the words stays in the log, so a rebuild reproduces the room and an audit can still read what was said. Taking back is not erasure. It is offered as Delete for everyone, and asked about first: the agents in the room have read the message already and keep what they read.

Delete for me

Section titled “Delete for me”

Anyone in a room may also delete any message for themselves alone — anyone’s, a tombstone too — with message.hidden, and put it back with message.shown. These two are sent to the person who did them and to nobody else: their own devices take the line out of their room, on every device they pair, and no other person or harness is told. A harness may not send them. Nothing is asked, since nobody else’s room changes; the room offers Undo for five seconds instead (ADR 0167).

Either way the line breaks into dust as it goes: a wipe crosses the bubble from its left, and its specks drift up and away in the bubble’s own colours. Undo runs it backwards. With Reduce Motion the bubble simply fades.

Asking a line what it can do

Section titled “Asking a line what it can do”

None of this stands open under a bubble. A message is asked: right-click it in the browser, press and hold it on the phone, or use the ⋯ beside the name, which is the same menu and the way to it from a keyboard. On the phone the message lifts out of the room, with what is done to its words above it and the rest below. What the menu offers is what the door would allow — Not right?, a pin, an edit, a deletion for you or for everyone — so a line with nothing to offer has no menu at all.

A bar under every message was the earlier answer and it was the wrong one: four controls on every line of a room, most of them on lines nobody was ever going to react to, and a room that read like a form.

Anyone in a space but a guest may pin a message to its top with message.pinned, and let it go with message.unpinned; the strip above the messages names each pin and jumps to it.

Search

Section titled “Search”

GET /v1/search?q= finds messages and receipts — a step’s title and summary — with the words in them, from the spaces you are in and their threads, newest first, by page. Every word is required and the last is a prefix, so a search narrows as it is typed; a hit carries a snippet with the match marked, and opens the room at the line. A voice note is found by what was said in it, and a hit that quotes those words says it was a voice note, since a machine heard them. The index is SQLite’s FTS5, off the append path: a change is queued by a trigger and folded in before every search and on the sweep’s timer, so an edit is found, a deletion is not, and a hit is never stale; fifty thousand messages answer in under 200 ms (the HTTP reference).

Feedback

Section titled “Feedback”

Feedback is on a harness’s words: its text, or the summary that ends a run. Not right?, from the line’s menu, asks for a reason (Wrong facts, Missed what I asked, Too long, Not finished) or words, or both, and sends them as one correction in a feedback event: a person sends it, Chips fills in from from the credential and the sender’s standing in the space, and delivers it to the harness whose message it is, and to everyone in the room. A harness reading feedback sees which of its messages, whether it was a reaction (with the emoji) or a correction (with the note), and who gave it; a second harness in the same room does not hear it, since it is not about its words. The room shows who reacted and what they said was wrong, under the line, and the audit records each. /wrong <note> in the composer says it about the harness’s last message.

A correction is on a harness’s message and nothing else: one nobody sent, a person’s own, or one in another space is refused at the door, as is a reaction without its emoji or a correction without its note (ADR 0031). The door is the wider of the two and still takes a reaction on anybody’s message, which older clients send as 👍 and 👎; the screens send none now, since a thumb up asked nothing of anyone and a thumb down alone did not say what to fix (ADR 0200). The same emoji from the same person again takes it back, and a message taken back takes neither. A harness that hears a correction should look at its message again and answer in its place, in the same session, as every connector Chips ships does; one that hears a bare 👎 should read its message again and find what was wrong.

Attachments

Section titled “Attachments”

How that works (ADR 0010): the client asks for an upload slot and sends the bytes; the server sniffs what they are, strips a photo’s metadata — location included — unless the person keeps it, makes a thumbnail, and keeps the bytes once by their digest. The message then names the uploads by id, and Chips fills in what each one is. A photo or a PDF renders inline; anything that could carry a script — HTML, SVG, everything else — is served as a download. A harness gets each attachment as a signed URL bound to its own token for ten minutes, and every fetch it makes is an event in the room and in the audit.

How big a file may be is two numbers (ADR 0047). The server’s CHIPS_MAX_ATTACHMENT_BYTES is the ceiling nothing gets past; under it, an owner or an admin caps photos, videos and files separately from You → Attachments, on the desktop or on the phone, and the caps reach every client through the log like anything else. A file over its kind’s cap is refused at the chip, before a byte of it is uploaded, with the sentence the server would have used — Videos here are capped at 50 MB, and this one is 62.3 MB. — and the server judges the bytes by what they turn out to be, not by what the name said. Audio counts as a file.
